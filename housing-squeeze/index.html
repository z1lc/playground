Housing · Czechia

Czechia’s housing squeeze shows up in prices, not in rents or crowded homes

Since 2015, inflation-adjusted house prices in Czechia have risen by 57%, more than in any of 21 rich-country peers except Portugal. The usual symptoms of a shortage are muted. Official rents have not outpaced inflation, and fewer young adults live with their parents than a decade ago. The supply side helps explain why the pressure has gone into prices.

Housing · United States

America’s housing squeeze shows up in rents as well as prices

Since 2015, inflation-adjusted rents in the US have risen by 17% on the official measure, faster than in any of 20 rich-country peers except Ireland. Real house prices are up 54%. Yet the US has added homes about as fast as its peers, and faster than its population has grown. The squeeze is clearer in household budgets than in national housing totals.

Housing stock and population in Czechia have both grown a little more slowly than in the typical peer country. What sets it apart is the price of buying a home. Real rents on the official index ended 2025 slightly below their 2015 level, while real house prices rose by more than half.

The US sits close to the middle of the pack for growth in housing stock and population. It stands out on cost. Real rents are up 17% since 2015, second only to Ireland, and real house prices are up 54%, the fourth-biggest rise among 22 countries.

In Spain, the shortage shows up as young adults who cannot afford to leave home. Czechia has gone the other way. The share of 25- to 34-year-olds living with their parents peaked at 34% in 2015 and has since fallen to 22%, well below the EU average. In neighbouring Poland and Slovakia it is more than half.

Living at home has become more common among young Americans, but from a low base. The share of 25- to 34-year-olds living with their parents rose from 11% in 2005 to a peak of 18% in 2020, and was 16% in 2025. Comparisons are rough: each country measures this differently, and the figures for Canada, Australia and Japan come from censuses and surveys held every five years.

Almost all of Czechia’s population growth since 2015 has come from migration, above all the arrival of refugees from Ukraine in 2022. Deaths now outnumber births by about 36,000 a year, so without migration the population would be shrinking. Even so, the number of dwellings has grown faster than the number of residents.

Unlike Czechia, the US still records more births than deaths, though natural increase has shrunk to about 519,000 a year. Most recent growth came from immigration. Net arrivals totalled 6.7 million in the three years to July 2024, then fell to 1.3 million in the following year. Even so, the number of homes grew faster than the population over the decade.

The national totals hide where and how fast homes get built. Czechia completes about three homes per 1,000 residents a year, fewer than most peers and well under half of Austria’s rate. Before the World Bank stopped measuring, getting a construction permit in Prague took 246 days, longer than anywhere else here except Slovakia and Canada.

Permitting is where policy is now moving. A new Building Act took full effect in July 2024, but its digital permit system malfunctioned badly and the minister responsible, Ivan Bartoš, was dismissed that September. In September 2026 the lower house overrode the Senate to pass an amendment that moves permitting from municipal offices to a new state authority. Prague’s Metropolitan Plan, which replaces a zoning plan from 1999, has applied since 1 September. The government aims for about 50,000 new dwellings a year by 2030. In 2025, 33,430 were completed.

The US completes about 3.8 homes per 1,000 residents a year. That is more than Czechia, but well below Canada and Australia, whose populations have grown much faster. On the World Bank’s measure permits were quick, at 81 days in New York and Los Angeles, faster than everywhere here except Denmark and Finland. That survey timed a standard warehouse permit, though, not the zoning approvals that new housing often needs.

Estimates of the national shortfall range from 3.7 million to 5.5 million homes, according to a Congressional Research Service summary. Completions reached 1.63 million in 2024, the most since 2006, before slipping to 1.49 million in 2025. In July 2026 the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act became law without the president’s signature. It speeds federal environmental reviews, funds zoning reform and bars large institutional investors from buying more single-family homes for 15 years.
